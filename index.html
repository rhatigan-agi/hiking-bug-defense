01The doctrine

Three corrections to how almost every “bug protection” list is built

Most bug guides — including the two drafts this one replaces — are about what to wear while walking. That's maybe half the problem. When people say bugs ruin nature for them, they rarely mean the walking. They mean the stop (lunch, the summit, the view you hiked four miles for, where you're stationary and get eaten), the bad twenty minutes (the boggy col, the black fly cloud, the stretch you just endure), and the aftermath (the week of itching that makes you not want to go back).

A clothing plan addresses none of those three. That's why so many plans feel thorough and the hikes still feel awful.

Treat the clothing as a passive barrier you set up once and forget. Spend your actual attention — and most of your money — on the stop, the bad twenty minutes, and the aftermath.

Second correction: don't put your weakest agent on your most-attacked ground. A recurring move in bad guides is botanical oils and folk remedies on the head, ears and neck. But black flies — the signature New England misery — specifically target the hairline, ears and collar, and greenheads and deer flies orbit your head before landing. That zone needs the strongest thing you own, plus mesh.

Third: this is a seasonal and regional problem, not a constant one. New England doesn't have “bug season.” It has five or six overlapping seasons with different enemies, and they don't line up between the Allagash and the Connecticut shoreline. The kit for June 5th in Baxter is not the kit for October 20th in Litchfield County.

Four things fall out of this chart:

  • June is the worst month in northern New England, by a wide margin. Peak black flies land directly on top of peak nymphal ticks. If you only fully kit up for part of the year, make it mid-May through early July.
  • Late July into early August is the worst window on the coast — greenhead peak, deer fly peak, mosquito peak, all at once. Inland is measurably kinder that fortnight.
  • The autumn adult tick wave is the one that catches people. Bugs are “over,” so nobody treats clothes or checks — and adult deer ticks are out on any day above roughly 40°F, right through November. This is also the season where heavy tight-woven pants like denim are genuinely the right tool.
  • Black flies are a fixed, short, predictable window. Roughly mid-May to the end of June, later at elevation. They bite in daylight only, peaking around 9–11 a.m. and 4–7 p.m. You can schedule around them — hike midday, hike a ridge, or hike in July.

03Regional field notes

New England is not one bug region. It's five, and they need different kits.

The North Woods

Northern ME · Northeast Kingdom VT

The worst black flies in the eastern United States, full stop. Baxter, the Allagash, the 100-Mile Wilderness in June are a genuinely different category of experience — and the mosquitoes that follow in July are the closest thing the Lower 48 has to the Alaskan interior. Maine's Lyme rates are now also among the highest in the nation, so this is not a misery-only zone.

This is the only region that justifies the full armor loadout below. Head net is not optional in June.

The Mountains

White Mtns NH · Green Mtns VT · Western ME

Black flies concentrate in the notches and along running water — they breed in streams, not ponds, so the brookside trail is the bad one and the dry ridge is the good one. Above treeline in the Presidentials the wind usually ends the problem entirely. Both states carry high Lyme incidence at lower elevations.

Use elevation and wind tactically. The bug problem is often solvable by route choice alone.

Southern New England

CT · RI · Eastern MA

The Lyme epicenter — the disease is named for Lyme, Connecticut. Milder winters mean a longer tick season rather than a worse one, with adults active in many winter thaws. Black flies are a minor concern here. The emerging story is the lone star tick pushing north along the coast, now established on Martha's Vineyard and Nantucket and moving into Bristol and Barnstable counties.

Lower mesh needs, higher tick discipline. Check year-round, not seasonally.

The Salt Marsh Coast

Cape Cod · Plum Island · CT & RI shoreline

Greenhead flies own late July and the first week of August. They breed in the marsh grass, hunt by sight in daylight, bite hard, and shrug off repellent. The control program's traps are literally black boxes on legs built to mimic the silhouette of a grazing animal — which tells you exactly what attracts them, and exactly what not to look like.

Wear light colors, stay out of the marsh at peak, and know that no repellent solves this one. Mesh and timing do.

The Islands

Martha's Vineyard · Nantucket · Block Island

Disproportionately high tick-borne disease for the acreage, and the front line of the alpha-gal problem — Martha's Vineyard logged more than 500 positive alpha-gal tests in 2024. Babesiosis is notably concentrated here as well.

Treat island hiking as maximum tick discipline regardless of how benign the landscape looks.

04Six threats, six different answers

Organizing a bug plan head-to-toe is the common structural mistake. The countermeasures differ by insect, not by body part.

Blacklegged / deer ticks Ixodes scapularis

The main event. New England carries some of the highest per-capita Lyme rates in the country, plus anaplasmosis and babesiosis. Ticks don't fly, jump, or drop from trees — they sit on low brush and leaf litter with their forelegs out and grab you as you brush past. Transmission generally requires 24–48 hours of attachment, which is the single most useful fact in this document.

CounterPermethrin on fabric, a sealed ankle, and above all the post-hike check. Because attachment time is the variable, finding them the same day beats repelling them.

Lone star ticks Amblyomma americanum

Newer, and behaviorally different in a way that matters: they don't just wait, they actively hunt — they'll detect you and crawl toward you across open ground. Spreading north along the coast with the warming climate. They carry ehrlichiosis and, notably, alpha-gal syndrome, a lasting allergy to red meat and dairy triggered by a sugar in their saliva.

CounterSame permethrin discipline, but don't assume standing still on a clear patch is safe ground. In southern and coastal New England, treat every break spot as contested.

Black flies Simulium spp.

The northern New England signature. They breed in running water, so they're worst near streams and in the notches. They do not bite through clothing — they crawl into openings: collar, cuffs, hairline, ears, sock line. Drawn to CO₂ and dark colors, grounded by wind above roughly 10 mph, and fondest of humid, overcast, calm days.

CounterMechanical, not chemical. Seal every opening and carry a head net. Repellent helps at the margins; mesh ends the argument.

Mosquitoes Aedes & Culex spp.

The one where running hot genuinely matters — they track CO₂ from 30+ feet, then switch to heat and skin odor up close. They're weak fliers. Worst at dawn and dusk, near standing water, in still air. New England also carries a small but real EEE and West Nile risk in late summer, concentrated in southeastern Massachusetts.

CounterThe only threat where a skin repellent is the primary tool — and the only one that a breeze, a fan, or an area repellent meaningfully defeats.

Deer & horse flies Chrysops & Tabanus spp.

Largely immune to the whole repellent conversation. They hunt visually, are drawn to dark moving shapes, and orbit the highest point on you — your head — before committing.

CounterExploit the orbit. A sticky patch on the back of the hat crown catches them, because you are the bait and the trap sits above your ears. Effective, cheap, and it looks ridiculous.

Greenhead flies Tabanus nigrovittatus

The coastal specialist, and the one honest guides admit they can't fully solve. Salt marsh breeders, daylight hunters, persistent, and resistant to essentially every repellent. Season runs June to September with a savage peak in the last two weeks of July and the first week of August.

CounterTiming and mesh only. Avoid marsh-edge trails at peak, don't present a large dark silhouette, and accept that this is the one fight you win by not showing up.

05Terrain that does the work for you

Free protection, available by route choice. The most underrated section of any bug plan.

  • Wind is the best repellent ever invented. Above roughly 10 mph, black flies are grounded and mosquitoes can't hold a line. Exposed ridges, summits, lakeshores with a breeze, and anywhere above treeline are frequently bug-free while the valley below is unbearable.
  • Elevation sheds bugs. The Presidentials above treeline in June can be pleasant while Crawford Notch is a black fly nightmare. Climbing out of it is a legitimate tactic.
  • Know which water you're near. Black flies need moving water; mosquitoes need standing water. A rushing brookside trail is a black fly trail. A beaver pond or a boggy col is a mosquito trail. Dry ledge and open hardwood are neither.
  • Midday is the gap in northern New England. Black flies peak 9–11 a.m. and 4–7 p.m.; mosquitoes own dawn and dusk. Roughly 11 a.m. to 3 p.m. is the quiet window in black fly season.
  • Sun and dryness suppress almost everything except deer flies. Open ledge beats shaded understory for a lunch stop, every time.
  • After the first hard frost, the flying insects are done — but ticks are not. October and November are the best hiking of the year in New England and simultaneously a peak tick wave. Don't let the absence of mosquitoes tell you the season's over.

06The three moments

Where the misery actually lives, and the three purchases that address it

The stop — a portable bubble

This is the highest-leverage item in the plan and it's missing from most guides. A butane or rechargeable Thermacell vaporizes a pyrethroid into a roughly 15–20 foot bubble. In a peer-reviewed field trial it cut biting rates by a mean of about 92–93%, holding above 90% for six hours. It's useless while walking — it needs still air and a few minutes to establish — which is exactly why it's perfect for the part you're losing: lunch, the summit, the overlook, camp.

Usage detail that matters: light it the moment you stop, not after you start getting bitten. It needs about ten minutes to build the zone.

The bad twenty minutes — mesh, reachable in five seconds

A head net weighs an ounce, costs about eight dollars, and is the only thing that reliably ends a black fly encounter. The reason people own one and still suffer is storage: if it's buried in the pack, you'll tough it out rather than stop and dig. Put it in a shoulder-strap or hip-belt pocket where it comes out without breaking stride. And it must be held off your skin by a stiff 3-inch brim, or black flies simply bite through the mesh where it touches.

The aftermath — treat the reaction, not just the bite

For people who hate bugs, the lasting damage is usually the histamine reaction, not the bite — black fly bites in particular can itch for weeks on sensitive skin. A thermal bite pen (a ~51°C pulse that disrupts the local histamine response) works well if used within minutes. Pair it with an oral antihistamine and 1% hydrocortisone. This is the cheapest part of the kit and probably does the most for whether you want to go out again next weekend.

On heavy cotton and denim

Denim's tight weave genuinely defeats mosquito mouthparts, and light-wash denim shows nymphs well. But it's heavy cotton: it soaks sweat, stops breathing, dries slowly, and chafes wet. For anyone who runs hot, in July, it raises the exact thermal signature you're trying to hide. In the mountains there's also a real safety edge — wet cotton at elevation is how people get hypothermic on 50°F days.

The split that keeps the preference intact: treated nylon from May through September, denim in the October–November adult tick wave, where it's cool enough that the breathability penalty vanishes and the tight weave is pure upside. Jeans, in the season they're actually the right tool.

08Tier 3 — full armor

The maximalist loadout. Overkill for a Connecticut day hike; correct for Maine in June.

There is a point — the Allagash in June, a bog crossing in the Northeast Kingdom, a still humid evening in the North Woods — where no repellent regime is sufficient and the answer is total mechanical exclusion. The good news is that a full bug suit is light, cheap relative to most outdoor gear, and packs to about the size of a rain shell.

Where the evidence is strongest

In the controlled trial, permethrin-treated sneakers and socks made subjects 73.6× less likely to be bitten by a tick — compared with 4.74× for treated shorts and just 2.17× for a treated T-shirt. A whole treated outfit came in at 3.36× overall. If you treat one thing in your entire kit, treat your feet and ankles. The armor above is built outward from that fact.

The practical compromise: keep the bug jacket and gloves permanently stuffed in a pack pocket from mid-May to mid-July. You will wear them perhaps three times a season, and on those three days they will be the difference between turning around and finishing the hike.

09Packs, and the pouch that makes this work

Two things about the packs themselves that almost no guide mentions:

  • Treat the shoulder straps and hip belt with permethrin. They're fabric, they press against you all day, and they wipe brush at exactly tick height. Nobody treats them. Never put DEET on straps or buckles — it degrades nylon and plastic.
  • Buy light-colored packs. Same reason as light clothing — you can see what's crawling on them. Dark packs also read as attractive targets to deer flies and greenheads, which hunt large dark silhouettes.

The bug pouch

One small zip pouch per person, about sandwich-bag size, that lives permanently in the pack lid and gets restocked — never unpacked. The whole system fails when the pieces are scattered across two packs and a glovebox.

  • Picaridin lotion, travel size · DEET pump in June
  • Fine-point tweezers · Tick Key
  • Zip bag and a Sharpie for the tick you pull off — date it, keep it
  • Cetirizine, hydrocortisone, a few alcohol wipes
  • Deer fly patches, July

The head net does not go in the pouch. It goes in an outside strap pocket where you can reach it without stopping. That distinction is the entire reason people actually use them.

Keep a contractor trash bag in the car as well. Hiked-in clothes go straight into it at the trailhead and straight to the dryer at home — they never touch the house, the couch, or the laundry pile.

10The protocol

A genuine sequence — each step depends on the one before it.

  1. Night before

    Verify, don't re-treat

    Check the permethrin date written inside each garment. Home spray is good for roughly 6 weeks or 6 washes; if it's past, treat tonight, because it needs to dry fully. Check the forecast: humid, overcast and calm is a black fly day; wind over ~10 mph grounds them.

  2. Trailhead — 5 minutes

    Skin, seal, stage

    Picaridin on neck, ears, jawline, hands and wrists. Sunscreen first, repellent second, never a combo product. Socks over the cuffs or gaiters on. Head net into the strap pocket. Deer fly patch on the hat crown if it's July.

  3. On trail

    Walk the center, sit on foam

    Stay off the brushy margins — that's where ticks are questing. Never sit directly on logs or leaf litter; that's what the pads are for. Light the Thermacell as you stop, not once you're being bitten. Quick visual leg-and-partner check at each break.

  4. Car, before driving

    Strip the obvious

    Lint-roll pants and socks. Outer layer into the contractor bag. This is the step that keeps nymphs out of the car seats and out of the house.

  5. Home — within two hours

    Dryer first, then wash

    Ten minutes on high heat kills ticks; washing does not reliably do so — they survive cold and even warm cycles. So the dryer comes before the washer, not after.

  6. Same day

    The full-body check, mutually

    Shower, then check each other. Hit the spots people skip: scalp and hairline, behind and inside the ears, armpits, navel, waistband line, groin, behind the knees. Nymphs are the size of a poppy seed. This step is where the 24–48 hour window is actually won — everything upstream of it just improves your odds.

  7. If one is attached

    Pull, save, date, watch

    Fine-point tweezers as close to the skin as possible, steady upward pull — no twisting, no petroleum jelly, no match. Drop it in the zip bag with the date. Watch for rash or fever for 30 days. Given New England's rates, it's worth asking your doctor in advance about single-dose doxycycline prophylaxis, which has a 72-hour window — you want to know the plan before you're standing there with a tick in a bag on a Saturday.

11For the tinkerer

Four real upgrades, roughly in order of payoff per hour spent

1. The soak method — double your permethrin's life

Instead of spraying, dilute a permethrin concentrate and soak garments in a sealed bucket or bag. It saturates fibers far more evenly than surface spray and lasts roughly twice as long — about 12 washes versus 6. It's also dramatically cheaper per garment, and it's the only practical way to treat something as thirsty as denim or a pack harness.

Dilution safety — read before buying concentrate

Concentrates ship at wildly different strengths — 10% is the hiker standard, but agricultural bottles run 36.8%. Follow the specific bottle's label for the dilution ratio; never carry a ratio over from a different product. Target working strength is 0.5%.

Wet permethrin is highly toxic to cats, and to fish and bees. Dry treated items away from any cat, and pour spent soak liquid down a household drain — never a storm drain, never outdoors near water.

2. Send your clothes out for factory treatment

The best answer for heavy garments. Insect Shield runs a mail-in service that factory-treats your own clothing with a bonded process rated for about 70 washes, versus 6 for home spray. Mail in the jeans, both sets of hiking clothes, and any removable pack straps once, and the permethrin-maintenance chore disappears for years. Highest-leverage tinker move on this page.

3. Trial a frontier repellent against your picaridin

Two EPA-registered actives outside the DEET/picaridin duopoly:

  • 2-undecanone (BioUD), derived from wild tomato. A 7.75% formulation is commercially available for skin and gear; its label claims about 5 hours on mosquitoes and 2 on ticks. In published comparisons it outperformed DEET against both blacklegged and lone star ticks — genuinely interesting, and easy to A/B test with one of you on each.
  • Nootkatone, from grapefruit and Alaska yellow cedar, developed by the CDC and EPA-registered in 2020. Consumer products have been slow to materialize, so treat this as one to watch rather than buy.

4. Small mechanical experiments

  • A clip-on battery fan at rest stops. Mosquitoes are famously weak fliers; moving air defeats them without chemistry. Complements the Thermacell rather than competing with it.
  • Drop the fragrance chain. Unscented soap, shampoo, deodorant, and detergent for hiking clothes. Also worth knowing: beer consumption measurably increases mosquito attraction — relevant to the post-hike patio, if not the trail.
  • Log it. You'll be running several variables at once. A note on your phone after each hike — date, trail, what you wore, what bit you, how bad — will tell you in one season which parts of this are doing the work. Most of the bad advice in this field exists because nobody keeps records.

12The skip list

Widely repeated advice that doesn't survive contact with the evidence

RecommendationWhy it's out
Vanilla extract on the earsFolklore Not EPA-registered, no robust evidence as a standalone repellent. Vanillin is a fixative — it slows other oils evaporating — which is why products like Bug Soother pair it with lemongrass. Worse, it puts your weakest agent on the zone black flies attack hardest.
Repellent oil along the hat brimInvented mechanism There is no “downward vapor curtain.” Oil of lemon eucalyptus is a legitimate registered skin repellent, but it's volatile, short-lived, off-label on fabric — and it's an eye irritant you'd be placing directly above your eyes, where sweat carries it down.
Mirrored lenses to break insect trackingNo basis No entomological support for the “hollow cavity” theory. Dark, shiny, moving objects do draw deer flies and greenheads, but that's visual hunting and mirroring doesn't help. Wear wraparounds for branches and repellent drip — good reasons, different ones.
“Cotton traps heat, intensifying your CO₂”Wrong premise CO₂ output is metabolic. Fabric doesn't change how much you exhale. Cotton is poor hiking fabric for real reasons — weight, wet, warmth — so the conclusion survives, but don't reason from this.
Petroleum jelly, matches, or twisting a tick outActively harmful All of these can make the tick regurgitate into the bite, which is the opposite of what you want. Steady upward pull with fine-point tweezers, nothing else.
Bug guides citing shopping linksNot sources A recurring tell: recommendations footnoted to Google Shopping product URLs, Facebook posts, Reddit threads, or pest-control company blogs. When a guide's format demands a mechanism for every item, a mechanism gets generated whether or not one exists.
What conventional advice gets right — keep all of it

Permethrin on fabric at 0.5%. Picaridin at 20% on skin, and its plastic-safety advantage over DEET. Loose fit over tight knits. Light colors for spotting nymphs. Socks over the cuffs. And saturating the footwear — the best-supported single action in the entire field.
